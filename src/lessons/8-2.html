Two different protections

Confidentiality is a lawyer's ethical duty to protect all information about a client's matter. Attorney-client privilege is a rule of evidence. It keeps private communications between a client and lawyer, made to get legal advice, out of court. Sharing privileged material with outsiders can waive the privilege, which means losing it.

Where AI creates risk

  • The tool keeps or learns from what you type. Then client information could show up in the company's systems or, in rare cases, in other users' answers.
  • People at the company can read it. Some companies review chats for safety.
  • Someone else can demand it. Stored chats can be requested in a lawsuit or by the government.
  • Clients use AI on their own. In United States v. Heppner (2026), a federal judge held that a defendant's own chats with an AI tool were not privileged.[[3]] The lesson Privacy and your data explains the case.

What the ABA says

ABA Formal Opinion 512 says lawyers should understand how a tool uses the information they put in. Before putting confidential information into a tool that may learn from it or share it, lawyers generally need the client's informed consent. General standard wording in an engagement letter is not enough.[[1]] Florida's Opinion 24-1 also says lawyers should get informed consent before disclosing confidential information to a third-party AI tool.[[2]]

A practical checklist

Use only tools your employer has approved for client data. Read the terms about training, data storage and access. Remove names and identifying details when you can. Get informed consent when the rules require it. Warn clients that their own AI chats about the case may not be private.
