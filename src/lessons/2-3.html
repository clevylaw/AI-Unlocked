Who owns what AI makes

In the United States, copyright protects work made by people. In March 2025, a federal appeals court said so plainly in Thaler v. Perlmutter.[[1]] An AI system cannot be the author of a work under the Copyright Act. In March 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear the case, so that ruling stands.[[3]] The court did not decide how much a person must add to an AI-made work for it to be protected. Other countries have their own rules.

What AI companies may train on

Many lawsuits ask whether AI companies may train their systems on other people's work without permission. One early ruling came from inside the legal world. In February 2025, a federal judge in Delaware ruled in Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence. Ross had used Westlaw headnotes, the short case summaries Westlaw editors write, to build a competing legal research tool. The judge rejected Ross's fair use defense, which allows some uses of copyrighted work without permission.[[2]] In September 2026, the federal appeals court for that region agreed.[[4]] It noted that Ross's tool found cases but did not write new text, so the ruling may not settle cases about chatbots. Other cases are still being decided, so the law here is unsettled.

Deepfakes and scams

AI can copy someone's voice from a short recording. Law firms handle client money and wire transfers, which makes them targets. A caller might sound exactly like a partner or a client.

Protect client money

Never move money because of a call, voice message or email alone. Confirm by calling back on a number you already have on file. Use a second person to approve any change to payment details.

Say when you used AI

Many courts, schools and employers now have rules about using AI and saying when you did. Check the rules that apply to you before you use it for coursework, a court filing or client work.
