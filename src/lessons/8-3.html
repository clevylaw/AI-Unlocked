The duty that does not change

When you sign a court filing, you vouch for it. In federal court, Rule 11 says your signature means you looked into it with care and the law supports your arguments. The ethics rules also forbid false statements of law or fact to a court. It makes no difference whether you, a colleague or an AI wrote the first draft.

This is why the lawyers in Mata v. Avianca were sanctioned.[[3]] The fake cases were bad enough. They also failed to come clean when the court asked about them.

Judges' and courts' own AI rules

In May 2023, Judge Brantley Starr, a federal judge in Texas, became one of the first judges to make a rule about generative AI. Lawyers had to file a signed statement. It said either that AI drafted no part of the filing, or that a person checked the AI-drafted parts against books or trusted legal databases.[[2]]

The court later made a rule for every case. Under its Local Civil Rule 7.2(f), a brief prepared with generative AI must say so on its first page, under the heading "Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence." The judge can ask which parts AI prepared. If a brief leaves out that statement, the lawyer is treated as saying no part of it was prepared with AI.[[1]] Judge Starr no longer asks for his own separate statement.[[2]]

Many other judges and courts have issued their own rules, and they differ. Some ask you to disclose AI use, some ask you to certify that you checked it, and a few limit it.

Before every filing

Check the court's local rules, which apply to every case there. Then check the judge's standing orders, which are that judge's own rules. Look for anything about AI. Follow them exactly. If you used AI, keep a note of what you used it for and how you checked the result.

If you find a mistake after filing

Correct it quickly. The duty of candor requires lawyers to fix false statements they have made to a court. In Mata v. Avianca, the court stressed that the lawyers made things worse by standing by the fake cases after the court questioned them.
