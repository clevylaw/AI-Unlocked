Check your school's rules first

Law schools and professors set their own rules on AI. Some allow it for study but ban it on exams and graded writing. Some require you to say when you used it. Breaking these rules can be an honor code violation, which can follow you into the bar's character and fitness review. Read your school's policy and each syllabus.

Good ways to study with AI

  • Explain a rule at your level. Say what you already know and what confuses you.
  • Teach it back. Explain a rule in your own words, then ask the AI to point out mistakes and gaps.
  • Practice with hypotheticals. Ask for short made-up fact patterns and test yourself.
  • Turn your own notes into flash cards or a quiz.

Sample study prompt

"I'm a first-year student. I understand that a contract needs an offer and acceptance, but I'm confused about the mailbox rule. Explain it with one everyday example. Then ask me two questions to check I understand."

Where it can mislead you

AI can state a rule from the wrong state, mix up the majority and minority rules, or summarize a case wrongly. A case summary is not the case. Read the assigned cases and check AI explanations against your casebook, your professor's materials or a trusted study aid.

Study tools made for your own files

Some tools answer questions using only the documents you upload, such as your class notes. One example is Google's Gemini Notebook, which was called NotebookLM until July 2026. Only upload material you are allowed to share.
