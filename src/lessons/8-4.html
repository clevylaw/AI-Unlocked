Why a routine matters

AI mistakes in legal research look exactly like real research. A made-up case has a plausible name, a real-looking citation and a convincing summary. A real case can be cited for something it never said. The Stanford study of legal research tools found both kinds of errors.[[1]] Only a steady checking routine catches them.

A checking routine

  1. Find it. Pull every case, statute and rule from a trusted legal database or an official court or government website. If you cannot find it, do not use it.
  2. Read it. Read the full passage you rely on. A summary is not enough. Make sure it supports your point.
  3. Check it is still good law. Run it through a citator such as Shepard's or KeyCite. Check that statutes and rules are the current versions.
  4. Check the details. Compare every quote, page number, court and date with the original.
  5. Check where it applies. Make sure your court must follow the authority, or at least is likely to find it persuasive.

Do not ask the AI to check itself

Asking "Is this case real?" proves nothing. The AI can confirm a fake case as confidently as it invented it. Use sources outside the AI.

Good uses of AI in research

AI is useful for getting oriented in an unfamiliar area, suggesting search terms, listing issues to consider and summing up a case you have already found and read. Treat its suggestions as leads to follow, never as authority.

Supervising research by others

If a junior lawyer, paralegal or student researcher used AI, ask how they checked the results. Your signature on the filing makes the research yours.
