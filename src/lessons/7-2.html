Stage 1: Pretraining

The model learns to predict the next token across trillions of tokens of text and computer code. This takes thousands of special computer chips running for months. The result is a base model. It knows a lot, but it only continues text. It does not follow instructions yet.

Stage 2: Learning from example chats

Next, the base model trains on many example conversations that show helpful answers. This teaches it to act as an assistant. It learns to follow instructions, answer questions and use a steady format.

Stage 3: Learning from feedback

People compare pairs of answers and pick the better one. The model is then trained to give the kind of answers people picked. This is called reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. It makes answers more helpful and polite. It can also make the model eager to please, so it may give an answer when it should say "I don't know."

Stage 4: Learning to reason

Thinking models get extra training on problems with answers that can be checked, such as math and code. They are rewarded when the final answer is correct. This helps with multi-step problems. Legal answers are harder to check automatically, so this training helps less with legal judgment.

Knowledge cutoff

A model only knows what was in its training data. That data stops at a date called the knowledge cutoff. A new statute, rule change or appeals decision after that date has to come from a search, a legal database or documents you give it.

Open and closed models

Some companies publish the trained settings of their models, called weights. Anyone can then run and change those models. These are called open-weight models, such as Llama, Mistral, DeepSeek and Qwen. An organization can run them on its own computers, which keeps client data inside. Other companies only offer their models through their own apps and services.
