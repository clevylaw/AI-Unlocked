You already use AI

When your email moves junk mail out of your inbox, that is AI. When a legal research site suggests other cases you may want to read, that is often AI too. So is the software in a big lawsuit that sorts millions of emails into "likely relevant" and "likely not relevant."

All of these do the same kind of job. A computer makes a prediction, which is a careful guess. It bases that guess on patterns it found in lots of examples.

Definition

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a computer system that does jobs that usually need a person's thinking. Sorting documents, understanding speech and writing a first draft are all examples.

Courts have accepted AI for years

In 2012, a federal judge in New York approved a method called predictive coding in the case Da Silva Moore v. Publicis Groupe.[[1]] It is known as the first time a court approved this method. Lawyers marked a sample of documents as relevant or not. The software learned from those choices and ranked the rest. Today this is often called technology-assisted review, or TAR.

What AI is not

Films often show AI as a robot that thinks like a person. Real AI has no feelings or opinions, and it does not know that it exists. It owes no duty to a client, and no bar can discipline it. It is a machine that is good at finding patterns. You stay responsible for the work.

Example

A review tool does not "know" that an email is about a price-fixing deal. It learned which words and patterns showed up in the emails lawyers marked as relevant. Then it looks for those patterns in new emails.

Why AI is suddenly everywhere

People have been building AI since the 1950s. It got much better in the last 15 years. The internet gave computers billions of pages to learn from. Computer chips became much faster. Researchers also found better ways for computers to learn from examples. The most important of these is called deep learning. You will meet it in the lesson Types of AI.
