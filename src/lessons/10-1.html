Where AI helps litigators

  • Document review. Tools rank or sort documents by how likely they are to be relevant or privileged. Courts have accepted this kind of review since Da Silva Moore in 2012.[[1]]
  • Summaries. Deposition transcripts, hearing transcripts and large email sets can be summed up quickly.
  • Timelines and chronologies. Pulling dates and events from many documents into one table.
  • Drafting. First drafts of routine motions, discovery requests and letters to opposing counsel.

The risks that matter most

  • Invented or misread authority. The sanctions in Mata v. Avianca came from a brief.[[2]] Every citation in a filing must be found and read.
  • Misstated testimony. A summary can put words in a witness's mouth or give the wrong page and line.
  • Missed documents. A review tool can miss relevant or privileged documents. Check its work with sampling, and agree on the method with the other side where needed.
  • Confidential and protected material. Documents produced under a protective order may only be used and shared as the order allows. Check before loading them into any AI tool.

Court rules on AI

Some courts now require a disclosure when a brief was prepared using generative AI. One example is the Northern District of Texas's Local Civil Rule 7.2(f).[[3]] Check the local rules and the judge's standing orders before every filing.

Discovery about AI

AI also shows up as evidence. Parties may ask for the prompts and outputs someone created, or for information about how a company's AI tool made a decision. Think early about where your client's AI chats are stored and whether they need to be preserved.
