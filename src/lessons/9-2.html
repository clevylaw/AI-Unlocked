Start with the problem

Pick one task that takes a lot of time and has a clear right answer, such as first-pass contract review or summing up deposition transcripts. Buying a tool because it is popular, without a specific task in mind, rarely works.

Questions to ask the vendor

  • Data. Do you train on our data? How long do you keep it? Where is it stored, and who can see it?
  • Security. What security checks and certifications do you have? What happens if there is a data breach?
  • Accuracy. How do you test accuracy? On what kinds of tasks? How often are citations wrong?
  • Sources. Where does the legal content come from, and how current is it?
  • Control. Can we limit who sees which matters? Can we export or delete our data?
  • Cost. How is it priced, and how will we explain any costs to clients?

Get it in writing

Put promises about data use and security in the contract. Involve whoever handles your IT security and your ethics or risk questions.

Run a small trial

Test the tool with a small group, using made-up documents or documents you are allowed to use. Compare its results with work by experienced people. Count the time spent checking its output as well as the time it saves. Decide ahead of time what would make you stop.

After you adopt it

Write a short policy on what the tool may and may not be used for. Train people on how to check its work. Review the policy as the tool and the rules change.
