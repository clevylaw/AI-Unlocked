How courts use AI

Courts use AI for jobs such as translating, summing up filings, research and answering the public's questions on court websites. Many now have written rules. In California, every court that allows generative AI must have a use policy. The policy must forbid putting private court information into public AI tools. It must also require staff to check AI output for mistakes.[[1]] The federal courts issued interim guidance in July 2025 that also says to check AI output.[[2]]

Two federal court orders (D.N.J. and S.D. Miss. 2025)2

In 2025, orders from two federal judges, one in New Jersey and one in Mississippi, contained made-up quotes and other errors. Both judges told the Senate Judiciary Committee that someone on their staff, an intern in one case and a law clerk in the other, had used AI. Both added new review steps.

The lesson for lawyers is the same as for judges. Read every case and quote in an order that affects your client. If you find an error, raise it through the proper channel, such as a motion to correct.

People without lawyers

Many people go to court without a lawyer, often because they cannot afford one. A 2022 study by the Legal Services Corporation looked at people with low incomes. For 92 percent of the serious civil legal problems they had, they got no legal help or not enough.[[3]]

AI chatbots can explain court forms and steps in plain language at any hour, which can help. But they make the same mistakes for everyone, and people without legal training are less able to catch them. Researchers also warn of a two-tier system, where people who can pay get lawyers and everyone else gets only AI.[[4]]

Help versus legal advice

In most states, only licensed lawyers may give legal advice. Doing it without a license is called the unauthorized practice of law. Whether an AI tool that tells someone what to do in their own case crosses that line is still unsettled. Courts and bars are working on it.

If you help people without lawyers

  • Point them to the court's self-help center, official forms and legal aid.
  • Tell them to check every case, rule and deadline an AI gives them on an official court website.
  • Remind them not to put other people's private information into a public chatbot.
  • If you are not their lawyer, say so clearly.
