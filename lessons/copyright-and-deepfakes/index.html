Copyright, deepfakes and scams

Courts are still working out who owns what AI makes and what AI companies may train on. Fake voices and videos add new risks.

2 min read · 3 min practice

Who owns what AI makes

In the United States, copyright protects work made by people. In March 2025, a federal appeals court said so plainly in Thaler v. Perlmutter.1 An AI system cannot be the author of a work under the Copyright Act. The court did not decide how much a person must add to an AI-made work for it to be protected. Other countries have their own rules.

What AI companies may train on

Many lawsuits ask whether AI companies may train their systems on other people’s work without permission. One early ruling came from inside the legal world. In February 2025, a federal judge in Delaware ruled in Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence. Ross had used Westlaw headnotes, the short case summaries Westlaw editors write, to build a competing legal research tool. The judge rejected Ross’s fair use defense.2 Other cases are still being decided, so the law here is unsettled.

Deepfakes and scams

AI can copy someone’s voice from a short recording. Law firms handle client money and wire transfers, which makes them targets. A caller might sound exactly like a partner or a client.

A good rule for any firm

Never move money because of a call, voice message or email alone. Confirm by calling back on a number you already have on file. Use a second person to approve any change to payment details.

Say when you used AI

Many courts, schools and employers now have rules about using AI and saying when you did. Check the rules that apply to you before you use it for coursework, a court filing or client work.

For technical readers (optional)

In Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence, the court found infringement of 2,243 Westlaw headnotes and weighed the market effect most heavily, because Ross was building a competing product. The ruling involved a non-generative search tool, so its reach to generative AI training is debated. Many other cases against AI companies are still in progress, and some have reached different results on fair use. Check the current state of the law in your jurisdiction before you advise anyone.

Try it yourself

Open ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini and paste in this prompt.

Someone in my office got a voice message that sounds like our managing partner, asking for an urgent wire transfer to a new account. Give me a short checklist to decide whether it might be a scam using an AI copy of a voice, and the steps to take before any money moves.

Check what you learned

In Thaler v. Perlmutter (2025), what did the federal appeals court decide?

Answer: B. The court held that the Copyright Act requires a human author, so an AI system cannot be listed as the author. It did not decide how much human input is enough for protection.
