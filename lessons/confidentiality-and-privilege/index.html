Confidentiality and privilege

Client information is the thing lawyers must protect most. AI tools raise new questions about where that information goes.

2 min read · 3 min practice

Two different protections

Confidentiality is a lawyer’s duty to protect everything about a client’s matter. Attorney-client privilege is a rule about evidence. It keeps private talks between a client and a lawyer, made to get legal advice, out of court. If you share privileged material with outsiders, you can waive the privilege, which means you lose it.

Where AI creates risk

  • The tool keeps or learns from what you type. Then client information could end up in the company’s systems. In rare cases, it could show up in other people’s answers.
  • People at the company can read it. Some companies review chats for safety.
  • Someone else can demand it. Stored chats can be requested in a lawsuit or by the government.
  • Clients use AI on their own. In United States v. Heppner (2026), a federal judge held that a defendant’s own chats with an AI tool were not privileged.3 The lesson Privacy and your data explains the case.

What the ABA says

ABA Formal Opinion 512 says lawyers should know how a tool uses what they type into it. Some tools learn from it or share it. Before you put a client’s secrets into one of those, you generally need the client’s informed consent, which means a clear yes after you explain the risks. Standard wording in an engagement letter is not enough.1 Florida’s Opinion 24-1 also says to get informed consent before sharing client secrets with an outside AI tool.2

A practical checklist

Use only tools your employer has approved for client data. Read the terms about training, data storage and access. Remove names and identifying details when you can. Get informed consent when the rules require it. Warn clients that their own AI chats about the case may not be private.

For technical readers (optional)

When you review a vendor, ask whether inputs and outputs are used for training, how long they are kept, where they are stored, who at the vendor can access them, what security certifications the vendor holds, which other companies process the data, and how the vendor responds to subpoenas and breaches. Get the answers written into the contract. Also check whether the tool keeps data from different matters separate, so ethical walls hold.

Try it yourself

Open ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini and paste in this prompt.

I’m reviewing an AI vendor’s terms for a law firm. Make a list of 10 plain-language questions to ask about how the vendor stores, uses and shares the information we type in. Include questions about training on our data, how long data is kept, who can see it and what happens if they get a subpoena.

Check what you learned

Under ABA Formal Opinion 512, what is usually needed before putting confidential client information into an AI tool that may learn from it?

Answer: C. The opinion says informed consent is needed before putting client information into a tool that may learn from it or share it, and that general boilerplate in an engagement letter is not enough.
