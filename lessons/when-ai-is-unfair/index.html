When AI is unfair

AI can treat some people unfairly even when nobody meant it to. In law, that can affect bail, sentencing and hiring.

2 min read · 3 min practice

Where bias comes from

AI learns from data made by people. That data reflects our history, including the unfair parts. If an AI learns from one-sided examples, it picks up the same slant. Then it repeats that slant for many people at once.

Risk scores in criminal cases

Some courts use software that scores how likely a defendant is to commit another crime. One tool is called COMPAS. In 2016, the news group ProPublica analyzed COMPAS scores and reported that Black defendants were more often wrongly labeled high risk than white defendants. The tool’s maker disputed the analysis.2

State v. Loomis, 2016 WI 68, 881 N.W.2d 7491

A defendant argued that using a COMPAS score at his sentencing violated his right to due process. The Wisconsin Supreme Court disagreed, but it set limits. Judges may consider the score as one factor, not the deciding one. They must also get written warnings about the tool’s limits. The U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear the case.

Hiring tools

Employers use AI to screen résumés and rank candidates. If a tool learned from a company’s past hiring, it can copy past unfairness. New York City’s Local Law 144 requires employers that use these tools for hiring or promotion to have a yearly independent bias audit and to publish a summary of the results. The city began enforcing it in July 2023.3

Why it matters for lawyers

AI now plays a part in decisions about bail, sentencing, jobs, loans and benefits. Lawyers may need to question how a tool works, ask for its testing results and argue about whether it treated their client fairly. Laws such as the European Union’s AI Act require extra checks for these high-risk uses.

For technical readers (optional)

Researchers have several exact definitions of fairness. One is equal accuracy for every group. Another is equal rates of false alarms, such as being wrongly labeled high risk. A third is that a given score should mean the same thing for every group. These definitions usually cannot all be true at once, which is part of why COMPAS was so hotly debated. A bias audit breaks a tool’s results down by group, for example by comparing selection rates by sex and race, to spot gaps.

In State v. Loomis (2016), what did the Wisconsin Supreme Court decide about COMPAS risk scores?

Answer: C. The court allowed judges to consider the score as one factor, but not as the deciding factor, and required written warnings about its limits.
