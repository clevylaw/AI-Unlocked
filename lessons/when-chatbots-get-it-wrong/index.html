When chatbots get it wrong

Chatbots can invent cases, quotes and facts and still sound sure of themselves. Lawyers have been sanctioned for trusting them.

3 min read · 3 min practice

What a “hallucination” is

When an AI states something false as if it were a fact, people call it a hallucination. It might invent a case, put a real case in the wrong court or make up a quote. This happens because the AI writes the words that sound most likely, and sometimes the most likely-sounding words are wrong.

Real cases where it went wrong

Mata v. Avianca, Inc., 678 F. Supp. 3d 443 (S.D.N.Y. 2023)1

Lawyers filed a brief in federal court that cited cases ChatGPT had made up. When the court could not find the cases, the lawyers did not come clean right away. In June 2023 the judge fined the lawyers and their firm $5,000.

Park v. Kim, 91 F.4th 610 (2d Cir. 2024)2

A lawyer cited a state court decision that did not exist. She said she had used ChatGPT to find it and did not check it. The federal appeals court referred her for possible discipline. It said lawyers must read the authorities they rely on to confirm they exist and support the point.

United States v. Cohen, No. 18-cr-602 (S.D.N.Y. 2023, 2024)3

Michael Cohen, a former lawyer, gave his own lawyer case citations he had found with Google’s Bard chatbot. He thought it was a kind of search engine. The cases were fake and ended up in a court filing. The judge did not impose sanctions but called the lawyer’s conduct negligent.

Legal research tools make mistakes too

Tools built for lawyers search real legal databases before they answer, so they invent less. They still make mistakes. A Stanford study published in 2024 tested leading legal research tools. It found that they gave false or misleading answers between 17 and 33 percent of the time.4

How to protect yourself

  • Read every case you cite. Pull it from a trusted legal database or the court’s website, and make sure it says what you claim.
  • Check that it is still good law, using a citator service such as Shepard’s or KeyCite.
  • Check every quote and page number against the original.
  • Use AI for first drafts and ideas. Then fill in the law yourself, from sources you have read.

A good rule

Treat AI research like a note from a new intern who has read a lot but never been to law school. It may point you in a useful direction. It is never the final word.

For technical readers (optional)

Hallucinations happen because training rewards text that sounds likely, and sounding likely is different from being true. Legal research tools reduce this by first searching a database of real cases and then asking the model to answer from what it found. This is called retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG. It helps, but it does not solve the problem. The Stanford study found that the legal tools it tested gave false or misleading answers between 17 and 33 percent of the time, including answers that cited real cases for points those cases do not make.

Try it yourself

Open ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini and paste in this prompt.

Give me three real U.S. court cases about a landlord’s duty to keep common areas safe, with citations. Then, for each case, tell me honestly how confident you are that it exists and says what you claim. (Afterward, look up each case yourself in a legal database or on a court website.)
