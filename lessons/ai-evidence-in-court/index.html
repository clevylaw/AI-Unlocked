Deepfakes and AI evidence in court

AI can make fake photos, audio and video that look and sound real. Courts are working out how to test evidence that may be fake or made by AI.

3 min read · 3 min practice

Two kinds of AI evidence

Researchers who study this problem sort AI evidence into two kinds.3

Evidence everyone agrees AI made
For example, an AI-enhanced video, an AI analysis of business records or an AI-made recreation of an accident. The question is whether it is reliable enough to use.
Evidence one side says is fake
One side offers a photo, recording or message as real. The other side says AI made it. The question is whether it is real.

The rules today

Under Federal Rule of Evidence 901, the side offering evidence must show it is what they say it is.1 For a recording, that often means a witness who knows the voice or was there. Many states have similar rules. The current rules already apply to AI evidence. Judges can ask for expert witnesses, hold a hearing before trial and keep out evidence that would mislead the jury more than it helps.

New rules under study

The federal committee that drafts the rules of evidence is studying two new rules.2

  • Proposed Rule 707 would set a reliability test for AI-made evidence offered without an expert witness. A version was published for public comment in 2025. In 2026, the committee withdrew it to work on a revised version.
  • A draft Rule 901(c) would deal with deepfakes. First, the side challenging an item would need enough evidence that it could be a deepfake. Then the side offering it would have to show it is more likely real than not.

As of October 2026, neither rule is in effect. Check the current federal rules and your state’s rules before you rely on either.

What to do in practice

  • Ask clients early where each photo, recording and message came from and who has had it.
  • Keep the original files and their metadata. A screenshot or a forwarded copy is much harder to test.
  • If you offer an exhibit that AI enhanced or made, say so and be ready to explain how it was made.
  • If you think the other side’s evidence is fake, raise it early and consider a forensic expert.

Calling real evidence fake

Deepfakes also make it easier to wave away real evidence by calling it fake. Scholars call this the “liar’s dividend.”4 A claim that evidence is fake needs a good-faith basis, like any other claim you make to a court.

For technical readers (optional)

Detection tools look for signs of editing, such as odd lighting, sound that does not match lip movements, or missing camera data, called metadata. They can be wrong in both directions, so courts rely more on expert testimony and on proof of where a file came from and who handled it. Some cameras and apps can now attach content credentials, a signed record of how a file was made and edited. A file without them is not proof of a fake.
