Privacy and your data

What you type into an AI tool may be saved, read or used for training. That matters for you and even more for your clients.

3 min read · 3 min practice

Your chats may be saved and used

What you type into a chatbot may be saved. Depending on the app and your settings, staff may read it to check for safety problems. It may also be used to train future AI. A company may have to hand chats over to the government or in a lawsuit.

What to do

Open the privacy settings in each AI app you use. Most apps let you stop your chats from being used for training. Many also let you start a temporary chat that is not saved, and delete your history.

A court ruling on AI chats and privilege

In February 2026, a federal judge in New York ruled in United States v. Heppner.1 The defendant had used an AI chatbot on his own to work through his legal situation, and later shared the results with his lawyers. The judge held that those chats were not protected by attorney-client privilege, the rule that keeps private talks between a client and lawyer secret. The chatbot is not a lawyer, and the company’s privacy policy allowed it to share data. The chats were not work product either, because no lawyer directed them.

The ruling did not decide what happens when a lawyer directs the AI use or uses a closed business tool. Other courts may see it differently. It is a strong reason to warn clients that their own AI chats about their case may be seen by the other side.

Never share these with a public chatbot

  • Client names, case details or privileged documents.
  • Passwords, PINs or security codes.
  • Bank, card or government ID numbers.
  • Anyone else’s private information without permission.

Work and school accounts

Paid business plans usually protect data better. For example, they often promise not to train AI on it. If your workplace gives you one of these accounts, use it for work, and follow its rules.

For technical readers (optional)

Check whether a provider offers zero data retention, which means it does not keep what you send, and whether the contract promises not to train on your data. Check where the data is stored and who at the company can see it. For highly sensitive matters, options include removing names and identifying details before sending, running an open model on your organization’s own computers, or a private setup from a cloud provider. Prompts may also be saved in your own systems’ logs, which can be subject to discovery.

Try it yourself

Open The AI app you use most and paste in this prompt.

Open the app’s settings. Find out whether your chats are used for training, how long they are kept, how to start a temporary chat, and how to delete your history. If you can’t find these settings, ask the chatbot: “Where are your privacy settings, and what does each one do?”

The AI tools page links to each app. Most have a free plan.

Check what you learned

In United States v. Heppner (2026), what did the court decide about a defendant’s own chats with an AI tool?

Answer: B. The judge held that the defendant’s chats with the AI tool, made on his own and not at his lawyer’s direction, were not privileged and were not work product.
