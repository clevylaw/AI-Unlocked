Tokens, context and chance

Three basic ideas explain most of how a language model behaves, including its odd mistakes.

Tokens, not words

Models do not read single letters or whole words. Text is split into tokens. A common word is usually one token. A rarer word is split into several pieces. In English, 1,000 tokens is about 750 words.

"unbelievably helpful"  →  ["un", "believ", "ably", " helpful"]   (a made-up example)

This explains a well-known mistake. Ask a model how many r's are in "strawberry," and it may count wrong. It sees a few tokens, while you see ten letters. Newer models handle this better, often by working through the word step by step.

The context window

A model can only consider a limited amount of text at once. That text includes your instructions, the whole conversation, any documents and the answer it is writing. All of it must fit in the model's context window, which is measured in tokens. Current models hold from about a hundred thousand to more than a million tokens. When a conversation grows too long, apps drop or shorten the older parts. That is why a long chat can "forget" things from the start.

Chance and temperature

At each step, the model gives every possible next token a chance of being picked. Then one token is chosen. A setting called temperature controls how adventurous that choice is. Near zero, the model almost always takes the most likely token, so its answers stay the same. Higher settings give more variety. That is why the same prompt can give different answers.

What this means for cost

AI services for programmers, called APIs, charge by the million tokens. Tokens you send in and tokens the model writes have separate prices. Tokens the model writes usually cost several times more, so long answers cost more than long questions.

Try it yourself

Open ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini and paste in this prompt.

How many times does the letter r appear in "strawberry"? Then explain, using the idea of tokens, why language models used to find questions like this hard.
