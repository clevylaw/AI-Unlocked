Tokens, context and chance

Three basic ideas explain most of how a language model behaves, including some mistakes that matter in legal work.

3 min read · 3 min practice

Tokens, not words

Models do not read single letters or whole words. Text is split into tokens. A common word is usually one token. A rarer word, such as a party’s name or a Latin phrase, is split into several pieces. In English, 1,000 tokens is about 750 words.

"res ipsa loquitur"  →  ["res", " ips", "a", " loqu", "itur"]   (a made-up example)

This explains a well-known weakness. A model sees chunks, while you see letters and digits. It can miscount letters or slip on a digit in a long number, such as a docket number or a dollar amount. Always check numbers, dates and citations against the source.

The context window

A model can only consider a limited amount of text at once. That text includes your instructions, the whole conversation, any documents and the answer it is writing. All of it must fit in the model’s context window, which is measured in tokens. Current models hold from about a hundred thousand to more than a million tokens, which can be several long contracts. When a conversation grows too long, apps drop or shorten the older parts. That is why a long chat can “forget” a fact from the start.

Chance and temperature

At each step, the model gives every possible next token a chance of being picked. Then one token is chosen. A setting called temperature controls how adventurous that choice is. Near zero, the model almost always takes the most likely token, so its answers stay much the same. Higher settings give more variety. That is why asking the same question twice can give two different answers.

What this means for cost

AI services for programmers, called APIs, charge by the million tokens. Tokens you send and tokens the model writes have separate prices. Sending a 300-page contract with every question gets expensive, which is one reason legal tools search for the relevant parts first.
