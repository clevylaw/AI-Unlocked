Courts, candor and AI rules

Courts expect the same accuracy whether a person or a machine wrote the first draft. Some judges have added their own AI rules.

The duty that does not change

When you sign a court filing, you vouch for it. In federal court, Rule 11 says your signature means the legal arguments are supported by the law after a reasonable inquiry. The ethics rules also forbid false statements of law or fact to a court. It makes no difference whether you, a colleague or an AI wrote the first draft.

This is why the lawyers in Mata v. Avianca were sanctioned. The fake cases were bad enough. They also failed to come clean when the court asked about them.

Judges' own AI rules

In May 2023, Judge Brantley Starr of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas issued a standing order on generative AI. Lawyers had to file a certificate saying either that no part of their filing was drafted by generative AI, or that a person checked any AI-drafted language against print sources or legal databases. Many other judges and courts have since issued their own rules. They differ. Some ask you to disclose AI use, some ask you to certify that you checked it, and a few limit it.

Before every filing

Check the court's local rules and the assigned judge's standing orders for anything about AI. Follow them exactly. If you used AI, keep a note of what you used it for and how you checked the result.

If you find a mistake after filing

Correct it quickly. The duty of candor requires lawyers to fix false statements they have made to a court. Courts have generally been much harsher on lawyers who hid or defended fake citations than on those who owned up.

For technical readers (optional)

In federal court, Rule 11 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure says that by signing a filing, a lawyer certifies that its legal contentions are warranted by existing law or a nonfrivolous argument, after a reasonable inquiry. Model Rule 3.3 forbids knowingly making false statements of law or fact to a tribunal and requires correcting them. Many state courts have their own versions of both rules.
