How AI learns

Nobody gives AI a full set of rules. It learns from examples, much like a new associate learns from marked-up drafts.

Learning from examples

A normal computer program follows rules that a person writes. For example, "If a deadline falls on a Saturday, move it to Monday." Someone has to think of every rule ahead of time.

AI works another way. You give it thousands of examples instead of rules. You might show it 5,000 contracts where a lawyer has marked each clause that limits liability. The AI then works out for itself what a limitation-of-liability clause tends to look like.

Think of it this way

Nobody gave you a rule book for spotting a weak argument. You read many briefs, saw what worked and got feedback. Over time you learned the patterns. AI learns in a similar way, but from far more examples and with no real understanding.

Training data

The examples an AI learns from are called training data. Their quality matters a lot. An AI that learned mostly from federal court opinions may be weaker on state law or on the law of other countries. An AI that learned from old contracts may miss newer clauses.

This is also how AI can become biased, which means unfair to some groups of people. If the examples are unfair or one-sided, the AI learns that too. You will see real legal examples in the lesson When AI is unfair.

Practice, millions of times

During training, the AI makes a guess and checks whether it was right. Then it changes itself a tiny bit and tries again. It does this millions of times. Each small fix makes the next guess a little better. Nobody writes the rules. They grow out of all that practice.

Example

In a large lawsuit, a lawyer reviews a few thousand documents and marks each one relevant or not. The review tool learns from those marks. It then ranks the other million documents, so people read the most likely relevant ones first.

For technical readers (optional)

Training tries to make a number called the loss as small as possible. The loss measures how wrong the model's guesses are. A method called gradient descent works out which way to nudge each setting to lower the loss, using a step called backpropagation. It then nudges all the settings a little, one small batch of examples at a time. Engineers keep some examples aside for testing. This shows whether the model learned the pattern or only memorized the answers, which is called overfitting. In e-discovery, the same idea appears as measuring recall (how many relevant documents were found) and precision (how many of the documents found were relevant) on a test sample.
