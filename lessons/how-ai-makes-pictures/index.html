How AI makes pictures and video

AI can now make photos and videos that look real. Knowing how it works helps you judge evidence.

2 min read · 3 min practice

From static to a picture

Most picture tools use a method called diffusion. In training, the AI takes real pictures and adds random dots, like the static on an old TV. It keeps adding dots until nothing is left but static. Then it practices the opposite. It turns static back into a clear picture, a little at a time.

Think of it this way

Picture a sandcastle washing away in the waves, one frame at a time. Now imagine watching that film millions of times. In the end, you could play it backwards and build a castle out of loose sand. Picture AI learned to build pictures out of static in a similar way.

Where your words come in

When you type a description, another AI turns your words into directions. The picture AI then cleans up the static step by step, moving closer to a picture that matches your words. Video tools do the same thing across many frames.

Why this matters for evidence

The result can look exactly like a real photo, voice recording or video. A deepfake is a fake photo, voice or video of a real person, made with AI. Deepfakes can appear in court as fake evidence. A party may also claim that real evidence is a deepfake, to cast doubt on it.

What careful lawyers do

They ask where a file came from and who has handled it. They look for the original file and its details, such as when and on what device it was made. They check for a content label that says how the file was made. When the stakes are high, they hire an expert to examine the file.

For technical readers (optional)

Most picture models run diffusion in a squeezed-down version of the image, called a latent space, instead of on every pixel. A text encoder turns your words into lists of numbers called embeddings. At each clean-up step, those numbers steer the picture. A standard called C2PA can attach signed “content credentials” to a file to record how it was made and edited. Some companies also hide invisible watermarks in AI-made media. Tools that claim to detect AI pictures are unreliable, so treat their answers as one clue, never as proof.

Try it yourself

Open ChatGPT or Gemini (both can make pictures for free) and paste in this prompt.

Make a simple, clearly labeled diagram of a four-way intersection with two cars, for a training slide about how to describe a car accident. Then tell me three reasons a court might not accept an AI-made picture as evidence of what happened.

The AI tools page links to each app. Most have a free plan.
